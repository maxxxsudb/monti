(A) Protection as deletion vs. requota. In Monty-type games, "the host may not open your door" can mean two different operations: deletion (the host keeps his plan and your door is simply struck out) or requota (the host keeps the number of doors he opens and moves the opening elsewhere). For any permutation-invariant host plan (P(open set S | car position) depends only on |S|) and your door's prior p0 not exceeding any other door's prior, deletion costs exactly 0; the whole classical gain 1/2 -> 2/3 comes from requota.

(B) Coin host with unequal priors. The host knows the car; over every goat door he flips a coin and opens it with probability q, never the car. The loss of winning probability caused by forbidding him to touch your door is
  sum over nonempty sets C of closed other doors of q^(N-1-|C|) (1-q)^(|C|-1) * max(0, min(q*P_C, (1-q)*(p0 - P_C))),
where P_C is the largest prior among the doors in C. Protection is free iff p0 <= every rival prior. With equal rivals the loss is < 1/y*, y* = -W_{-1}(-e^{-2}) ~ 3.146 (Lambert W), and the bound is sharp.

(C) Zero-value criterion for checking your own door. Unequal priors p_j, conditionally i.i.d. tests with likelihood ratio l, rivals already tested, M = max over rivals of p_j * l_j. The value of testing your door is E[ E_0 max(M, p0*l) - max(M, p0) ]; it is zero iff no atom of M lies strictly inside (p0*min l, p0*max l). For a binary test the value is a six-piece function of the prior ratio with kinks at l/h, l, 1, h, h/l; at equal priors the zero is isolated. With a walk-away option (+1/-2/0) the same check can be valuable under forced choice yet worthless with walk-away (priors 1/7, 3/7, 3/7, your door the outsider, test 2/3 vs 1/6: +5/108 forced, exactly 0 with walk-away although playing is profitable).

(D) Snapshot vs. failure order. Three flashlights are shuffled: one fully works, one has a dead lamp, one a dead battery. You keep your own flashlight's lamp and must take the battery from one of the other two. A perfect check of which of the two other flashlights works as a whole is worth exactly 0 for your assembly (1/2 with or without it, for any prior on your flashlight's type). If instead the parts are weak rather than dead (a weak lamp burns out before a weak battery runs down) and you watch which of the two goes dark first, taking that one's battery gives 2/3 — as much as full information.

(E) Weaker donor, better assembly. Same three types (works / bad lamp / bad battery). Your flashlight stays off; the other two run until the first one goes dark. Taking the battery of the one that went dark first makes your lamp work for the target time with probability 2/3; taking it from the one still lit gives 1/3, although both batteries are good with probability 2/3. With r = P(weak lamp fails before weak battery), the success rates are (1+r)/3 vs (2-r)/3; for 1/2 < r < 1 the first-dark battery is individually LESS reliable yet gives the MORE reliable assembly.
