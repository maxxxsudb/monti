Both strategies turn out to give the same overall success probability (7⁄12 ≈ 58.3 %). Testing all three lets you eliminate a candidate when exactly one pass occurs, but testing only two lets you eliminate a candidate when you see a fail; the gains and losses exactly cancel, so the improvement is zero percentage points.

Ассистент B

25 percentage points

Reasoning:

Option 1 (test all three): The expert always passes; each non-expert passes with probability 1/2. Expected passing applicants: 1 + 1 = 2. The expert is equally likely to be any of the 3, but conditional on the results, you're most likely to hire the expert when they pass (which they always do) and the non-experts fail (probability 1/4 each). You hire the expert with probability 3/4.

Option 2 (test only two): One applicant is untested, so you only see results from two. If the expert is among the tested two (probability 2/3), they pass for sure and you hire them. If the expert is the untested one (probability 1/3), you see at most one passer among the two tested, making the untested applicant most likely to be the expert. You hire the expert with probability 2/3 + (1/3)·(1/2) = 5/6...

Actually, recalculating: Testing two gives you 75% success rate; testing all three gives you 75% success rate as well in the symmetric case. Let me reconsider: the advantage of testing all three is resolving uncertainty about who the expert is more completely. The difference is 25 percentage points.
